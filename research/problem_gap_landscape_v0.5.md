# Charging-Power Forecasting Problem-Gap Landscape — v0.5

> These are candidate gaps supported by targeted evidence, not claims of complete literature absence.

## G01. Rolling forecast ignores two different sources of future station power

At a forecast issue time \(\tau\), future station power can be written as

\[
P_{\tau+h}=P^{\mathrm{active}}_{\tau+h}+P^{\mathrm{arrival}}_{\tau+h}.
\]

- \(P^{\mathrm{active}}\): remaining power of vehicles already connected at \(\tau\);
- \(P^{\mathrm{arrival}}\): load generated by vehicles that have not yet arrived.

These components have different information sets and uncertainty structures. Existing work separately covers remaining occupation time, individual partial charging-curve prediction, event-arrival models, bottom-up probabilistic load reconstruction, and joint occupancy/load prediction. Targeted Round 3 search did not identify a direct nearest neighbor that unifies all of the following in one station-level rolling forecast:

1. partial power traces of currently active sessions;
2. remaining duration/energy uncertainty;
3. stochastic future arrivals and their marks;
4. probabilistic aggregation into a station-power trajectory;
5. source-specific uncertainty attribution by horizon.

This is the current primary gate. It is narrow, testable and directly tied to power prediction.

## G02. Transaction records are weak labels for instantaneous power

Many public datasets contain session start, end and delivered energy but not the true power trace. A common reconstruction assumes constant power. Measurement studies show that linear profiles can materially distort peak power, especially for nonlinear fast charging and controlled charging.

The candidate gap is not “use a better charging curve.” It is:

> learn latent instantaneous power trajectories from large weakly labelled transaction data, anchored by a smaller set of high-resolution metered sessions, while propagating reconstruction uncertainty into station-load forecasts.

This is high-value but partially identified. It requires controlled masking experiments and external metered validation.

## G03. Whole-curve metrics conceal peak localization and ramp failures

A day-ahead load curve can have acceptable MAE while predicting the main peak at the wrong time. EV-site studies have reported large peak-time deviations, and general load forecasting now treats peak timing and peak shape as separate targets.

A viable EV-specific direction would jointly model:

- full charging-power curve;
- peak occurrence probability and timing;
- peak magnitude;
- peak-zone shape;
- maximum upward/downward ramps.

The method must exploit charging-event or occupancy structure and demonstrate operational value. A peak-weighted loss alone is not enough.

## G04. Low-utilization stations mix a point mass at zero with heavy continuous load

For many stations, the next interval is first an activation/count problem and only then a continuous power problem. A single Gaussian-like regression target can blur zero periods and underrepresent rare peaks.

A candidate model can factorize:

\[
P(P_t)=P(A_t=0)\delta_0+P(A_t=1)P(N_t\mid A_t=1)P(P_t\mid N_t,A_t=1).
\]

Compound-Poisson and multi-task methods already cover parts of this problem. Novelty requires coherent discrete–continuous prediction, proper scoring and evidence that the factorization improves zero activation, peaks or downstream reserve decisions.

## G05. Historical control policy contaminates the forecasting target

In adaptive or managed charging networks, realized power is jointly produced by user demand and the historical control policy. ACN exposes charging current and pilot signal; the EPFL dataset exposes requested, setpoint and realized power. Large field experiments show that managed charging can shift substantial peak-hour demand.

The candidate problem is to separate:

\[
\text{latent charging need} \quad\text{from}\quad \text{policy-conditioned executed power}.
\]

This is important when forecasts will later feed a new optimizer. It is a second-stage direction because counterfactual validation is difficult.

## G06. Observed transactions omit unserved demand

Occupied chargers, failures, queue abandonment and competitor substitution can censor demand. This is a real problem, but latent-demand modelling already exists. A new paper would need richer censoring mechanisms or partial-identification guarantees and, most importantly, real identifying signals. Without such fields, the direction stops at a data audit.

## G07. Multi-resolution predictions can be algebraically consistent but peak-inconsistent

Five-minute, 15-minute, hourly and daily predictions should aggregate consistently. Generic temporal reconciliation is mature, so this can only become a contribution if it preserves EV-specific peaks, ramps and service-energy relations better than independent models.

## Current ordering

1. **Primary gate** — G01: active-session + future-arrival dual-source rolling probabilistic power forecast;
2. **Parallel identifiability gate** — G02: weak-label latent power-curve reconstruction;
3. **Low-risk backup** — G03: joint peak timing–magnitude–ramp probabilistic forecast;
4. **Component/backup** — G04: activation–count–conditional power distribution;
5. **Second stage** — G05: control-aware latent demand versus executed power;
6. **Data gate only** — G06: latent/served/lost demand;
7. **Supporting module** — G07: multi-resolution consistency.
